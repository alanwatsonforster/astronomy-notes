# A Telescope for the Islas Rosenbaum

## Problem

Your current telescope is a 114 mm Newtonian with a focal length of 450 mm and so a focal ratio of about f/4 (that is, 450 mm divided by 114 mm). You have 10 mm and 25 mm eyepieces and a lightweight equatorial mount.

As far as visual observations of the planets are concerned, this equipment has three problems:

First, it does not give sufficient magnification. The magnification is the ratio of the focal length of the telescope to the focal length of the eyepiece. So, the best you can manage with your telescope is 45×. You really want about twice that.

You might think you could buy a 5 mm eyepiece, but economic options have very little eye relief and so are very difficult to use. Even your 10 mm eyepiece only has an eye relief of 6.5 mm, which makes it quite uncomfortable to use without glasses and impossible to use with glasses. Really, unless you spend hundreds of dollars, an 8 mm eyepiece is about as short as you can go.

You might think you could buy a Barlow lens, a beam expander that fits between the telescope and the eyepiece and effectively increases the focal length of the telescope and hence the overall magnification by a factor or 2 or 3, depending on the model. However, you’d then run into the second problem.

Second, all Newtonian telescopes are susceptible to alignment errors and really need collimating before each use. This is not difficult, but it’s a bit of a faff. Furthermore, fast Newtonians (i.e., ones with a small focal ratio) are much more sensitive to alignment errors than slow ones. Really, you don’t want a Newtonian to be much faster than f/8, and yours is f/4. So, even though a Barlow lens or a 5 mm eyepiece would give you the magnification you want, you would be looking at an aberrated image unless you achieved an exquisite alignment.

Third, your tripod is flimsy, which means the telescope bounces around, and this makes it difficult to collimate and focus.

I know these problems, because I have a 127 mm Newtonian on almost the same mount.

## The Rolls-Royce Solution $600

What would an ideal solution look like, if price were no option? It would be insensitive to collimation (or at least robust so that normal handling does not take it out of collimation). It would have a longer focal length. The telescope would be sufficiently light and compact and the tripod and mount would be sufficiently heavy and stiff that vibrations would be well damped. And it would need to be portable, since I presume you don’t want to build an observatory.

The best solution, I think, is a 5-inch catadioptric telescope, a Schmidt-Cassegrain or a Maksutov-Cassegrain, on a better tripod and mount. For example, this one with manual controls at $600:

[https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-StarMax-127mm-Equatorial-Maksutov-Cassegrain-Telescope/rc/2160/p/9826.uts?keyword=127mm](https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-StarMax-127mm-Equatorial-Maksutov-Cassegrain-Telescope/rc/2160/p/9826.uts?keyword=127mm)

Or this one with computerized controls at $800:

[https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-StarSeeker-IV-127mm-GoTo-Mak-Cass-Telescope-Kit/rc/2160/p/114849.uts?keyword=127mm](https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-StarSeeker-IV-127mm-GoTo-Mak-Cass-Telescope-Kit/rc/2160/p/114849.uts?keyword=127mm)

I don’t think you need computerized controls for looking at planets, since they are bright, but people say they make the experience easier. Celestron sells an almost identical kit, undoubtedly made in the same factory in China.

These telescopes have a focal distance of 1540 mm, so you can get magnifications of about 60× (with the 25 mm eyepiece) or 150× (with the 10 mm). They are robust, and so tend not to go out of collimation. They are fairly light and compact, so tend not to vibrate so much.

However, I’m going to assume you don’t want to spend $600. So, what can you do?

## The Camry Solution $280

A more economical solution would be to get a 90 mm Maksutov-Cassegrain, like this one for $220:

[https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-Apex-90mm-Maksutov-Cassegrain-Telescope/rc/2160/p/9820.uts?keyword=90mm](https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-Apex-90mm-Maksutov-Cassegrain-Telescope/rc/2160/p/9820.uts?keyword=90mm)

You would need two accessories, first a 90 degree diagonal (otherwise you have to get your head under the telescope to look close to the zenith), like this one for $40:

[https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-125-Prism-Star-Telescope-Diagonal/rc/2160/p/8763.uts?keyword=diagonal](https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-125-Prism-Star-Telescope-Diagonal/rc/2160/p/8763.uts?keyword=diagonal)

And you would need this adapter to be able to mount it on your existing equatorial mount, something like this one for $22:

[https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-14-20-Adapter-for-EQ-2-Telescope-Mount/rc/2160/p/10101.uts?keyword=equatorial%20adapter](https://www.telescope.com/Orion/Orion-14-20-Adapter-for-EQ-2-Telescope-Mount/rc/2160/p/10101.uts?keyword=equatorial%20adapter)

I’d have to look more closely at your mount so see exactly which sort of adapter you need.

I also have some tips for stiffening the tripod, with rubber washers and clamps and other stuff you can buy in Home Depot for peanuts. I know this, because I have an almost identical tripod, and stiffening it has helped a lot.

That’s adding up to about $280.

## The Polo Solution $150

The cheapest solution would be to get a refractor with a long focal length. For example, something like this for $150:

[https://www.opticsplanet.com/meade-meade-polaris-70mm-german-equatorial-refractor-telescope.html](https://www.opticsplanet.com/meade-meade-polaris-70mm-german-equatorial-refractor-telescope.html)

And find a means to mount it onto your existing mount. This has a 900 mm focal length, so you’d get 90×. Refractors are robust to collimation, but being long are more susceptible to vibrations.
