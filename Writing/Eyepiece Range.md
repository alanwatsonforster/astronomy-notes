# Eyepiece Range

I am not alone in recommending that beginners start with the following eyepieces:

- 32 mm Plössl eyepiece
- Celestron 8–24 mm zoom eyepiece
- 2x Barlow lens

I recommend the Celestron Omni Plössls and Barlow lens, which are solid performers and not especially expensive. There are other eyepieces with equal or similar ranges, but the more expensive ones don’t seem to be much better and I have no experience with the cheaper ones. Again, the Celestron zoom is solid and inexpensive.

Here I’m going to attempt to justify this recommendation in terms of the field and magnification. Specifically,

- Does this set give an adequately wide field?
- Does this set cover an adequate range of magnification?

There will be math, but it will be only multiplications and divisions.

## Maximum Magnification

For a telescope with a focal length *F* and an eyepiece with a focal length *f*, the magnification is

*m* \= *F*/*f*.

If the aperture is *D* in mm, the magnification per mm is

*m*/*D* \= *F*/*fD* \= *N/f*,

in which *N* is the focal ratio *F*/*D*.

The maximum useful magnification is commonly considered to be 1–2× per mm (25–50× per inch) of aperture. We see that this is achieved by eyepieces with focal lengths between *N*/2 and *N* mm. For example, for an *f*/8 telescope, an eyepiece with a focal length of 8 mm will give a magnification of 1× per mm (25× per inch) and an eyepiece with a focal length of 4 mm will give a magnification of 2× per mm (50× per inch).

Given this, we can see the attraction of the combination of a 32 mm Plössl, an 8–24 mm zoom, and a 2× Barlow for a typical *f*/8 telescope:

- The 32 mm is an economical means to get a wide view.
- The 8-24 mm zoom covers magnifications up 1× per mm (25× per inch) of aperture.
- The 2x Barlow lens extends the zoom to magnifications of up to 2× per mm (50× per inch) of aperture.

If the telescope is faster than *f*/8, the highest magnifications won’t quite match these. For example, for an *f*/6 telescope, the highest magnifications will be about 1.5× per mm (38× per inch).

Note that the exit pupil diameter is *f*/*N*.
