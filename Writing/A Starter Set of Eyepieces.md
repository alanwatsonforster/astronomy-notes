# A Starter Set of Eyepieces

To make good use of a telescope, you need an adequate set of eyepieces giving a range of fields of view and magnifications.

Some telescopes are bundled with a decent 25 or 32 mm Plössl eyepiece, but any bundled eyepiece with a shorter focal length is likely to have inadequate eye relief and poor optical quality. As a consequence, you will normally need to buy some additional eyepieces.

Now, you can easily pay $100 or more for an eyepiece and might well want several, so this can be expensive. It doesn’t need to be, though. My recommended starter set of eyepieces for f/5 to f/11 telescopes will serve you well and costs only about $170. It consists of:

- Celestron Omni 32 mm Plössl eyepiece
- Celestron 8-24 mm zoom eyepiece
- Celestron Omni 2× Barlow Lens

With this set, you’ll be able to see just about anything that your telescope, observing conditions, and eyesight permit. More expensive eyepieces might allow you to see a little more detail, give a little more contrast, or give a more immersive view, but this set gets you into the game.

Here I will briefly describe the elements of the set and explain their roles.

## Why a 32 mm Plössl?

Good Plössl eyepieces combine low cost with very adequate contrast and sharpness over an apparent field (to the eye) of 50 degrees. The eye relief at longer focal lengths is adequate for comfortable viewing. The true field (on the sky) increases with the focal length of the eyepiece up to 32 mm, after which point wider fields are limited by vignetting in the barrel of the focuser.

A 32 mm Plössl is thus the cheapest way to get the widest true field possible with good sharpness, contrast, and apparent field.

It will often be the first eyepiece you use to find and center an object before switching up to higher magnification. It is useful for viewing larger objects such as the Pleiades cluster (M45), the Praesepe cluster (M44), and the Andromeda Galaxy (M31).

## Why an 8–24 mm Zoom?

The Celestron 8–24 mm zoom offers adequate performance and flexibility but has some disadvantages compared to conventional eyepieces. The sharpness is good in the center, but this does fall away noticeably at the edge of the field. The optics are fully multi-coated. The field of view is a generous 60 degrees at 8 mm, but falls to 50 degrees at 12 mm and a cramped 40 degrees at 24 mm. One strong point is that the eye relief is 15–18 mm, whereas Plössls and other simple eyepieces often have impossibly short eye reliefs at a focal length of 8 mm. Its role in this set is viewing smaller objects at higher magnification, and for this it is quite adequate, provided you don’t let the object drift too close to the edge of the field.

Let’s now consider the range of magnifications that are needed. A good rule of thumb for small telescopes is that one can often use magnifications up to 1× per mm (25× per inch) and occasionally use magnifications up to 2× per mm (50× per inch). For example, an 80 mm telescope can often be used with magnifications up to 80× and occasionally with magnifications up to 160×.

Let’s do the math, but don’t worry, it’s only multiplications and divisions. If the telescope focal length is *F* and the eyepiece focal length is *f*, then the magnification *m* is given by the ratio

*m* \= *F* / *f.*

Now, if we want magnifications up to 1× per mm of aperture and if the aperture is *D*, then we require

*m* \= D / mm

and so, combining these two equations, we see that we need the eyepiece to have

*f* \= (*F* / *D*) mm *\= N* mm,

in which *N* is the focal ratio of the telescope, the focal length divided by the aperture. What a beautifully simple result\! It says that to get a magnification of 1× per mm (25× per inch), we need an eyepiece whose focal length in mm is equal to the focal ratio of the telescope. For example, to get to 1× per mm with an f/8 telescope we need an 8 mm eyepiece, and to do the same with an f/11 telescope we need an 11 mm eyepiece.

Straight away, we see that an 8–24 mm zoom covers magnifications up to 1× per mm for a telescope whose focal ratio f/8 or slower (larger) and down to slightly more than the 32 mm. Most of the time, that’s the most useful range for observations.

You might think you can substitute the 8–24 mm with a set of Plössls. You can to some degree at the longer focal lengths, but at the shorter focal lengths it doesn’t work. The problem is the eye relief of Plössls scales with the focal length. It’s adequate at 15–32 mm, but a little tight at 12 mm, and too short at 10 mm or less.

## Why a 2× Barlow?

When conditions are good, you can push the magnification beyond 1× per mm. The 2× Barlow lens gives twice the magnification or, equivalently, half the eyepiece focal length. So, when it is used with the 8–24 mm zoom, it gives up to 2× per mm for any telescope that is f/8 or slower (larger).

For a telescope faster than f/8, say f/5 or f/6, then the combination of the 8-24 mm zoom and 2× Barlow won’t quite get to 2× per mm. However, it will get you most of the way there.

I note that some telescopes are bundled with a 2× Barlow. However, all the bundled Barlows that I have seen are optically awful. You will want to replace them with a higher quality lens, one with at least two elements and that is fully multi-coated.

## Cheaper Options

All eyepieces and Barlows at these prices are manufactured in China. Chinese companies such as SVBONY, Astromania, and GoSky sell apparently similar products to Celestron at about half the price. I worry a little about quality control and after-sales service, but they might be worth considering if funds are tight. Make sure the optics are fully multi-coated (and both “fully” and “multi” are important here) and the barrels are blackened on the inside.

If you see an apparently similar product for much less than about half the price of the Celestron equivalent, I’d suggest it is probably inferior, perhaps by not being fully multi-coated. I’d avoid these.

If you want Celestron’s quality but $170 is too expensive, I would recommend deferring the Barlow, which would bring the total price down to $135. Further, if your telescope came with a decent 25 mm Plössl eyepiece, and many do, you might be able to defer the larger field of the 32 mm Plössl. I did precisely this when I was starting and money was tight. My telescope came with a 25 mm Plössl, which I supplemented with an 8–24 mm zoom. Later I added a 2× Barlow and still later a 32 mm Plössl.

## Next Steps

These eyepieces will cover most of your needs at fairly good quality and a reasonable price, but they aren’t the last word.

The 32 mm has an adequate apparent field of view of 50 degrees, but many observers prefer wider fields. The 8–24 mm is sharp in the center, but the image degrades towards the edge of the field. Further, while the apparent field is a generous 60 degrees at 8 mm, it drops to 50 degrees at 12 mm, and a cramped 40 degrees at 24 mm.

As a next step, one might be tempted to supplement these eyepieces with a range of Plössl eyepieces of lower focal lengths. The Celestron Omni range includes 4, 6, 9, 12, 15 mm eyepieces each for about $30. Beware, however, that the eye relief of Plössls shrinks with the focal length. The eye relief is adequate at 15 mm and longer, but is right at the limit at 12 mm, and inadequate at shorter focal lengths. One can argue for adding 15 mm, 25 mm, or 40 mm Plössl eyepieces (especially since with 32 mm and the 2× Barlow they give 7.5, 12.5, 16, and 20 mm focal lengths). They would give better images towards the edge of the field and give wider fields than the zoom at 12–24 mm.

However, I think there are better options beyond this starter set. I’d recommend buying more sophisticated designs than Plössls. For example, the Celestron X-Cel LX line has wider apparent fields of view (60 degrees compared to 50 degrees) and adequate eye relief even at short focal lengths. The wider fields of view give a more immersive experience, allow you to see an object of a given size at a larger magnification or give you more time to view an object before it drifts out of the field. They typically cost about $100 each. There are more expensive lines of eyepieces that have even larger fields, and they are also worth considering.

## Slower Telescopes

Above I included the qualification that the 32 mm, 8-24 mm, and 2× Barlow were my recommendations for telescopes from f/5 to f/11.

For a slower telescope, such as an f/13 to f/15 Maksutov, I’d not recommend the Barlow, since the 8–24 mm zoom on its own gets quite close to 2× per mm. For such a telescope, my recommendation would be just:

- 32 mm Plössl $45
- 8-24 mm zoom $90
- TOTAL $135

I note that the Celestron C90 f/13 Maksutov comes with a fully multi-coated 32 mm Plössl, so in this case you’re off to a fine start and only need the 8–24 mm zoom (plus, probably, a 90-degree star diagonal).

I’d also note that slow telescopes are one place where you can get by with just Plössls and a Barlow lens. For example, this should work quite well:

- 32 mm Plössl $45
- 12 mm Plössl $30
- 2× Barlow $35
- TOTAL: $110

This gives 32, 16, 12 and 6 mm (the 16 and 6 mm from using the Barlow) and 2× per mm at f/12 and slower. If you use glasses, you’ll probably have to remove them to use the 12 mm.
