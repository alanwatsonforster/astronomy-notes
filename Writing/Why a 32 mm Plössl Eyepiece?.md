# Why a 32 mm Plössl Eyepiece?

A 32 mm Plössl 1.25-inch eyepiece often figures in sets of eyepieces recommended for people starting in visual astronomy. This eyepiece gives a large field of view on the sky and low magnification, so its roles are viewing large objects, viewing smaller objects in their context, and centering smaller objects before viewing them in more detail with other eyepieces.

Here, I’ll explain why this eyepiece is such a good fit in this role and mention some alternatives.

## Considerations

### True and Apparent Field of View

Let’s start the discussion by considering two different aspects of the field of view.

The *true field of view* is the diameter of the region being viewed *measured* *on the sky*. Large true fields of view allow you to take in a larger region of the sky, which is useful for observing larger objects such as the Pleiades, the Andromeda Galaxy, and others and also for finding smaller objects before viewing them at higher magnification.

The *apparent field of view* is the diameter of the region being viewed *as perceived by the eye*. It is larger than the true field of view because the optical combination of the telescope and the eyepiece produces a magnified view. For a given true field of view, an eyepiece with a larger apparent field of view will give a more magnified view and, subjectively, a more immersive experience.

The true fields of view of common combinations of telescopes and eyepieces range from about 0.25 to 5 degrees. The apparent fields of view of common eyepieces range from about 40 to 80 degrees.

The ratio of the apparent field of view to the true field of view is approximately the *magnification* of the combination of the eyepiece and telescope\[1\]. For example, an eyepiece that shows a true field of view of 2 degrees with an apparent field of view of 50 degrees will have a magnification of about 25\. Thus, for a given true field of view, an eyepiece with a larger apparent field of view will give a larger magnification.

We can now describe our requirements more precisely. We are looking for a low-cost eyepiece that will give a large true field of view (to see large objects) with an adequate apparent field of view (for a pleasing view).

### From Sky to Retina

In order to understand the limits on wide-field eyepieces, let’s consider the role of the telescope, eyepiece, and eye:

- The telescope forms a real image of the sky in its output focal plane.

- The eyepiece forms a virtual image of its input focal plane at infinity.

- The focuser is adjusted so that the output focal plane of the telescope coincides with the input focal plane of the eyepiece. Thus, the combination of the telescope and eyepiece forms a virtual image at infinity of the sky, but one that is typically brighter and magnified.

- The eye takes the image formed at infinity by the eyepiece and forms a real image of it on the retina, where it is detected by the visual system.

In most eyepieces the input focal plane is close to where the body joins the barrel\[2\]. This is not necessary from the point of view of optics — it would be quite possible to have the focal plane somewhat forward of this point — but this has become a de facto standard that helps maintain focus when changing from one eyepiece to another.

### Vignetting and Field Stops

The size of the image formed by the telescope is typically limited by *vignetting*, which occurs when the mechanical parts of the telescope block light from reaching the focal plane.

A typical amateur telescope has a focuser for 1.25-inch (31.75 mm) eyepieces. The size refers to the inner diameter of the draw tube or the outer diameter of the barrel of the eyepiece. Other less common sizes are 0.975-inch and 2-inch, and we’ll come back to 2-inch eyepieces later.

For a telescope with a 1.25-inch focuser, the diameter of the image in the focal plane is limited to 31.75 mm by the draw tube.

Worse, the focal plane is typically within the barrel of the eyepiece and the thickness of the barrel wall reduces the available diameter. Most 1.25-in eyepiece barrels have internal [M28.5x0.75 threads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_metric_screw_thread) for filters, and these have a clear diameter of about 27.7 mm.

Finally, manufacturers often install a field stop, a circular diaphragm in the input focal plane that limits the field seen by the eyepiece. These have three purposes. First, they limit the apparent field of view of the eyepiece to the field over which the optics give acceptable sharpness. Second, they prevent scattered light from entering the eyepiece (e.g, from grazing reflections in the barrel) and reducing contrast. Third, they give a sharp edge to the field of view, which many people prefer. If you look through an eyepiece, you can often see the edge of the field stop in focus right at the limits of the field.

### Requirements

So, getting back to our search for a low-cost wide-field eyepiece, we can now see that we want an eyepiece that combines:

- A field stop that is close to the maximum possible diameter;
- Optics that allow the whole field to be viewed with acceptable sharpness and an acceptable apparent field of view; and
- Low cost.

## Options

What are the options that fulfill these requirements? What are their advantages and disadvantages?

### 32 mm Plössl Eyepiece

Plössl eyepieces are the lowest-cost eyepieces that are widely regarded as having acceptable sharpness and acceptable apparent fields of up to 50 degrees. To match this apparent field to the largest field stop possible in a 1.25-inch eyepiece, we need to select an eyepiece focal length of about 32 mm. That’s it.

For example, the Celestron Omni 32 mm has a field stop that is 27.3 mm in diameter (only very slightly smaller than the 27.7 mm maximum diameter), an apparent field of view of about 50 degrees that encompasses this whole field. It weighs about 112 g (4.0 oz) and costs about $45.

### 25 mm Plössl Eyepiece

Many starter telescopes are supplied with a 25 mm Plössl eyepiece. It’s useful, but is not a complete substitute for a 32 mm Plössl, since its true field is significantly smaller, about 80% in diameter or 60% in area. This is because the 50 degree apparent field of the Plössl design requires a field stop diameter of about 22 mm at a focal length of 25 mm but allows a large field stop of about 27 mm diameter at a focal length of 32 mm.

The niche of these eyepieces is viewing objects that are slightly smaller than the maximum true field at a slightly larger magnification than that given by a 32 mm eyepiece.

If you have one of these eyepieces, then by all means use it instead of or in addition to a 32 mm Plössl, but if you don’t then there are probably better alternatives.

### 25 mm Eyepiece with a Wider Apparent Field

Another option is an eyepiece with a shorter focal length but with a more sophisticated design that allows a wider apparent field of view. Adding more elements to the design of the eyepiece allows acceptable performance over apparent fields of up to 100 degrees, albeit with increasing cost, size, and weight.

For example, the Celestron 25 mm X-Cel LX has a field stop that is 25 mm in diameter (only 9% smaller than the Celestron Omni 32 mm Plössl) and so covers approximately the same true field of view as a 32 mm Plössl. However, its wider apparent field of view of 60 degrees allows it to view the whole field stop with a higher magnification than a 32 mm Plössl. It weighs about 210 g (7.2 oz) and costs about $100.

An even better match is an eyepiece like the Explore Scientific 24 mm 68 series, which has a field stop that is 26 mm in diameter and an apparent field of 68 degrees. It weighs about 320 g (11.3 oz) and costs about $200.

These and similar eyepieces offer several advantages over a 32 mm Plössl. First, many people appreciate the wider apparent field of view. Second, the higher magnification shows more detail. Finally, the higher magnification darkens the apparent brightness of the sky by spreading the same amount of light into a larger area as seen by the eye. Since stars remain point-like, they maintain their brightness. The combination acts to increase the contrast of stars against the sky and permits fainter stars to be seen. This is especially desirable under light-polluted skies. Finally, being one or two steps up from the most basic range, they often offer improved mechanical build quality.

They do have one disadvantage, though. The same increase in magnification that darkens the brightness of the sky also darkens resolved nebulae. For bright nebulae, this is not a problem. However, it can be for the faintest nebular, especially in slow telescopes which give relatively dark images.

### 40 mm Plössl Eyepiece

Going in the other direction, a 40 mm 1.25-inch Plössl eyepiece will show the same true field of view as 32 mm 1.25-inch Plössl (since in both cases the field size is limited by the barrel), but will show it with lower magnification since the focal length is longer. This will lead to the apparent field of view being smaller.

For example, the Celestron Omni 40 mm eyepiece will show the same true field as the corresponding 32 mm eyepiece, but with an apparent field of about 43 degrees rather than 50 degrees and with a magnification about 80% lower. The price of a 40 mm Plössl is also about $45, the same as the corresponding 32 mm model. The weight is 160 g (5.6 oz).

Most people find an apparent field of view of 40 degrees to be somewhat cramped and prefer the wider apparent field of the 32 mm. Furthermore, the lower magnification of the 40 mm eyepiece will show less detail. For these reasons, the 32 mm is probably a better choice for a first wide-field eyepiece for general use.

However, one particular application in which a 40 mm Plössl does hold an advantage over a 32 mm eyepiece is increasing the brightness of diffuse nebulae. It does this by decreasing the magnification and so, effectively, squeezing the same amount of light into a smaller area as seen by the eye. The flip side is that the nebula is now smaller.

This advantage is especially useful in slower telescopes which intrinsically give higher magnifications and fainter images of diffuse objects, but it is potentially useful in any telescope slower than about f/4.5\[3\].

### 50 mm Plössl Eyepieces

Taking this to an extreme, Russell Optics make a [1.25-inch 50 mm Plössl eyepiece](http://russell-optics.com/quarter_inch.html) for $43 including shipping in the USA. This would give an even larger exit pupil and, in f/7 and slower telescopes, potentially brighter image than even a 40 mm eyepiece. However, the apparent field of view is probably about 32 deg, which might be claustrophobic.

### Both 25 mm and 40 mm Eyepieces

The best of both worlds might well be a 25 mm 60–70 degree eyepiece (for stars and bright nebulae) perhaps coupled with a 40 mm 40 degree Plössl (for faint nebulae).

This combination costs about $100-$200 for a 25 mm and $50 for a 40 mm, so it is not outlandishly expensive. However, it is much more expensive than a single 32 mm Plössl and a good fraction of the cost of a typical first telescope. It’s a solid upgrade, though.

### 2-inch Eyepieces

To obtain a wider true field, one needs to escape the mechanical limitations of a 1.25-inch eyepiece, for example by using 2-inch eyepieces.

The Celestron Omni 2-inch 56 mm Plössl eyepiece has a field stop that is 46 mm in diameter, and gives a true field that is wider (170% in diameter and 290% in area) than the previous options, albeit at lower magnification. Also, its image is brighter than the 25, 32, or even 40 mm eyepieces. It costs about $100.

However, to use 2-inch eyepieces, you’ll often need to change to a 2-inch focuser and also buy a 2-inch diagonal. These are good purchases for further down the line, but probably not appropriate for someone starting.

## Which 32 mm Plössl?

So which 32 mm should you look to buy? What characteristics are important?

It’s vital that the optics in the eyepiece be “FMC” or “fully multi-coated” to improve transmission, reduce scattered light, and improve contrast.  The words “fully” and “multi” are important, otherwise you can be almost sure that at least one surface will be either uncoated or only singly coated.

It’s also vital that the inside of the barrel be blackened to avoid glints. Grooving helps too.

Some people state that blackening the edges of the lenses is important too, but I’m not so convinced, since the edges are often hidden away behind the mounting rings.

Low-cost eyepieces are universally manufactured in China. Sometimes, what appears to be almost the same eyepiece is sold under different brands. If you buy a low-cost eyepiece from well-known brand such as Celestron, Explore Scientific, Sky-Watcher, or Orion you’re often mainly paying extra for quality control and customer service if there is a problem, although sometimes you also get nicer accessories such as caps. That’s the same in spades if you buy a premium Plössl eyepiece from TeleVue. If you buy from a Chinese vendor, you might well be getting a product of a similar quality at a lower price, but with that you need to factor in the possibility of lower quality control (which increases the probability of a problem) and worse customer service (which decreases the chance of getting it fixed).

The Celestron Omni 32 mm Plössls currently costs about $45. I have never read a word against it in reviews, and I am very happy with my example. Chinese vendors such as SVBONY, Astromania, and Gosky sell apparently similar eyepieces for about $20-$30. If you see an eyepiece at a lower price, I'd guess that it’s probably deficient in some way, perhaps by being only single-coated.
