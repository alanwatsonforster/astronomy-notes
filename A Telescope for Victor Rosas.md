# A Telescope for Victor Rosas

I’m sometimes asked to recommend an astronomical telescope for beginners.

## Binoculars

I sometimes recommend starting by using binoculars or a monocular. I have a 10×42 monocular that cost just US$55, and use it to view the Moon, the Pleiades (M45), the Galilean moons of Jupiter, the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), and the Orion Nebula (M42). Even though I have a telescope, the monocular still gets used just because of its sheer convenience.

If you can, use a binocular or monocular with at least 40 to 50 mm objectives (e.g., 8×40 as a minimum). Those with smaller objectives (e.g., 10×25 or 10×30) give fainter images.

However, with a magnification of only 8× to 12×, binoculars are not capable of viewing the rings of Saturn or the zones and bands on Jupiter, and don’t give much detail on lunar craters. For that, you really do need a telescope.

## General Considerations

Let’s start with some general considerations for a telescope for a beginner.

- The telescope should be low-maintenance. Newtonian telescopes (which have a mirror at the bottom of the tube and the eyepiece at the top) require optical alignment, and that’s not trivial for a beginner. So, this means the telescope should either be a refractor or a Schmidt-Cassegrain or Maksutov-Cassegrain.

- The telescope weight needs to be well within the capacity of the mount and tripod. If the telescope is too heavy, it will vibrate too much to be useful and flexure will make pointing very difficult. Unless you are prepared to spend US$400 or more on a mount, this really means the telescope should be less than about 2 kg.

- You can reduce vibration by keeping the tripod as low as possible. This can be achieved by not extending the tripod legs fully and sitting on a camping stool or chair to get your eye lower than when standing. However, a disadvantage is that this low position makes it difficult to use a straight-through finder telescope or red-dot finder, such as those typically supplied with cheaper telescopes.

- You typically view the sky near the zenith. This means that a 90 degree star diagonal is essential. Many terrestrial spotting scopes come with erecting 45 degree diagonals or erecting 90 degree diagonals, but these are not adequate for astronomical use. A 45 degree diagonal can’t easily be used close to the zenith and a cheap erecting 90 degree diagonal typically gives a low-contrast image.

- Pointing a telescope can be an issue for beginners. I’ve mentioned the problems with straight-through finder telescopes and red-dot sights. So, either the telescope needs to give a field of view of at least 2 degrees (which means the telescope focal length should be no more than about 800 mm for a typical 32 mm eyepiece with an apparent field of view of 50 degrees), or you need a laser finder, or you need to upgrade to a right-angle finder telescope.

- An equatorial mount, with slow-motion controls, is preferred over an altitude-azimuth mount, as it makes it much easier to center and follow an object. In an altitude-azimuth mount, the two axes move left-and-right and up-and-down. In an [equatorial mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_mount), the polar axis is tilted to point at the celestial pole and the other axis moves north-and-south. This means that you can follow an object as it moves across the sky by just moving the polar axis. Without moving the mount to follow an object, the sidereal motion will cause it to drift out of the field of the eyepiece in just a few minutes. Many people recommend that beginners start with an altitude-azimuth mount, as it is easier to set up. However, the advantage of an equatorial mount is that once you have found an object, it is easier to follow it. Given the difficulty beginners often encounter in finding objects, I think not losing them once they are found is more important. Furthermore, for visual observing, setting up an equatorial mount is actually very quick: just set the polar angle to the latitude (e.g., about 19 degrees in Mexico City) and then use the compass in your smartphone to point the polar axis to north. It doesn’t have to be precise.

- Many telescope kits come with inadequate eyepieces, with narrow fields of view and poor eye relief (i.e., you have to jam your eye right up against the eyepiece). Often, it will be worth replacing them with a wide-field 32 mm Plössl eyepiece and a 7–21 or 8–24 mm zoom eyepiece.

- The Moon through a telescope can be very bright\! A Moon filter reduces its brightness to a more comfortable level.

- If you want to view the Sun safely, to see sunspots, you need a solar filter.

- There are many smartphone apps to help you navigate the sky by using the smartphone’s inclinometers to show a map of the sky that moves as you move the phone. I use “Sky Walk 2”, but I don’t think it’s better or worse than many other options.

- A good place to see what to observe is Sky and Telescope’s “[Sky at a Glance](https://skyandtelescope.org/observing/sky-at-a-glance/)” articles.

## Recommended Option: Short-Tube 80 mm Refractor

My favored option is a short-tube 80 mm refractor on an equatorial mount with slow-motion controls. It will be fine for viewing brighter nebulae and galaxies, star clusters, sunspots, and Jupiter and Saturn. Its major advantage is that the wide field of view makes it easy to find objects and then the equatorial mount makes it easier to center and follow them; its major disadvantage is that bright objects such as the planets and the Moon will show some color fringing.

The “short-tube” part implies that the focal length is relatively short at only 400 mm. This gives a wider field of view than long-tube refractors of the same diameter, but this comes at the cost of more color fringing.

## Rejected Options

Now let’s consider some rejected options.

### Rejected Option 1: Long-Tube 80 mm Refractor

An example is the Celestron 80EQ refractor.

The “long-tube” part means that the focal length is relatively long at 880 mm. This means that even with a 32 mm eyepiece the field of view will only be about 1.8 degrees. That means that realistically, you’re going to need to use a finder, and that’s typically a struggle for beginners.

This problem could be alleviated by installing a laser finder, which typically cost about US$70. However, the cheapest kits (for example, the Celestron PowerSeeker range) have non-standard finder attachment points, which make this difficult.

You will also often need to replace the eyepieces and the diagonal.

### Rejected Option 2: Cheap 70 mm Short-Tube Refractor with an Altitude-Azimuth Mount

There are many, many kits with a 70 mm short-tube refractor similar to [this one](https://www.amazon.com.mx/CSSEA-Telescope-Astronomy-Adjustable-Educational/dp/B07F7ZR9NN/ref=sr_1_1?__mk_es_MX=ÅMÅŽÕÑ&crid=3CNK12JY6ZJ9B&keywords=70+mm+telescope&qid=1641184682&sprefix=70+mm+telescope%2Caps%2C145&sr=8-1) for MX$2600.

Having bought one myself, I don’t recommend them for several reasons.

- While the objective lens is typically not bad, the rest of the telescope is plastic and this leads to optical alignment problems and very poor images.
- The telescope is flimsy and altitude-azimuth, so you’ll have major problems with vibrations and with following an object as it moves across the sky.

Furthermore, the kit is not complete:

- They typically don’t have a 90 degree diagonal, so you’ll need to buy one (MX$900).
- The eyepieces are awful, so again you’ll need a decent 32 mm wide-field eyepiece (MX$1200) and 8–24 mm zoom (MX$2400).
- You’ll also need a lunar filter (MX$260).

The total, without a solar filter, is about MX$7400. Trust me, you’d be better off spending the extra MX$1400 to get my recommended 80 mm short-tube refractor.

I got one of these kits because I already had all the other items (mount, diagonal, and eyepieces) that this telescope needs to work adequately in theory, and even so I’ve spent more time trying to fix its problems than actually using it.

### Rejected Option 3: 90 mm Maksutov-Cassegrain Telescope

A 90 mm Maksutov-Cassegrain reflector (such as the Celestron C90 and similar offerings from Meade, Orion, and others) is a great light-weight, compact, low-maintenance telescope.

However, I can’t find packages that include both the telescope and an equatorial mount. Furthermore, given the longer focal length of these telescopes, the field is only about 1 degree with even a 32 mm eyepiece, so a laser finder will be necessary. Both of these factors are going to make this option more expensive. I’d suggest this is possibly a good second telescope.

The principal advantage compared to the recommended short-tube 80 mm refractor would be lack of color fringing on bright objects.

### Rejected Option 4: Newtonian Telescope

These require optical alignment, which is not a great experience for the beginner.

Again, the principal advantage compared to the recommended short-tube 80 mm refractor would be lack of color fringing on bright objects.

### Rejected Option 5: Any Larger Telescope

You may be tempted to get a larger telescope. However, it’s probably going to be too heavy for an entry-level mount, and so you’ll be fighting against vibrations and flexure. An 80–90 mm telescope is fine to start with, especially in typical urban or suburban conditions.

### Rejected Option 6: Any Smaller Telescope

By the time you’ve added the mount and the eyepieces, the savings from getting a smaller 60 mm or 70 mm telescope are negligible. An 80–90 mm telescope is really the sweet-spot.
